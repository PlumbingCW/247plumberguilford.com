How to Replace a Water Heater Relief Valve

The temperature and pressure relief valve, often called the T&P valve, is the safety device that opens if the tank gets too hot or the pressure climbs too high. It sits on the side or top of the water heater with a discharge pipe running down from it. If it drips, weeps constantly or has never been tested, it may need replacing, and replacing it is a short job when done with the water drained and the power off.

This guide explains what the valve does, how to test it, how the replacement goes and why some homeowners should call a plumber instead. It also covers the permit question in Guilford, which depends on what the work involves.

What the valve does and why it matters

Heating water in a closed tank raises pressure. A thermostat normally keeps the temperature in check, but if the thermostat or the burner fails, the water can overheat and the pressure can rise past what the tank was built to hold. The T&P valve opens to release hot water and steam before that point, which is why it is the last line of defense against a tank failure.

A discharge pipe attached to the valve carries released water to a safe place near the floor. It must not be capped, plugged or threaded at the end. A blocked discharge pipe defeats the whole purpose of the valve.

Guilford note: permits, licenses and scald safety

The Connecticut State Building Code exempts stopping leaks and repairing leaks in pipes, valves or fixtures from permits, but the exemption does not apply if the repair requires replacing or rearranging valves, pipes or fixtures. Replacing a relief valve is a valve replacement, so ask the Guilford Building Department what applies. It requires a valid state license and liability insurance from a contractor who applies for a permit.

The Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection says people doing plumbing, heating and similar work must hold the appropriate Connecticut license, and it does not name water heaters in its scope pages. If you are unsure who may work on your fuel-fired heater, ask the licensed contractor which class they hold and check it on the state lookup.

The replacement, in order

This is a general outline. Your model may differ, and the manual wins.

  • 1Turn off the power or the fuel supply. Switch the breaker off for electric heaters, and set a gas control to off or pilot.
  • 2Close the cold water valve and relieve pressure. Open a hot tap in the house, then lift the relief lever slightly to release pressure.
  • 3Drain a few gallons from the tank. Attach a hose to the drain valve until the water level is below the valve opening.
  • 4Remove the discharge pipe, then unscrew the old valve. Use a pipe wrench and note the rating printed on the valve.
  • 5Wrap the new valve threads with tape or sealant and thread it in. Hand tighten, then snug it with the wrench, without over-tightening.
  • 6Reattach the discharge pipe, refill and check for leaks. Run a hot tap until air is gone, then restore power or fuel.

Getting the rating and the discharge right

A replacement valve must match the tank. The valve carries a pressure rating, a temperature rating and a BTU capacity that must meet or exceed the heater. The tank's data plate lists the maximum working pressure. A valve with the wrong rating may open too early or fail to open in time.

The discharge pipe should be the same size as the valve outlet, run downhill without a trap and end a few inches above a floor drain or the floor. Copper, CPVC and other approved pipe are acceptable, and plain PVC is not for hot discharge. If the piping is awkward, a plumber can correct it as part of the replacement, and see our water heater repair page for what else is checked.

When to call instead

Call if the valve will not come off, or if the tank is a gas or oil unit and you are not sure how to shut down the fuel safely. Call if the tank is old and the valve is a symptom of something larger, or if you cannot get the pressure down. Scalding hot water is a real danger, and a rusted valve can break off and open the tank.

If the heater is more than ten years old and leaking at the valve, ask whether it is time for a water heater replacement instead.

Pressure, expansion tanks and why valves drip

A relief valve that drips after every heating cycle is often reacting to thermal expansion. When the house has a check valve or a pressure regulator on the water supply, the expanding water has nowhere to go and pressure rises until the valve opens. The right fix is usually an expansion tank, not a new valve, because a new valve will do the same thing.

You can check house pressure with a gauge that screws onto an outdoor spigot. Normal household pressure is generally in a moderate range, and a reading near the valve's opening pressure is a red flag. A plumber can test it quickly and set the right pressure for your fixtures.
