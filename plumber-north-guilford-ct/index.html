No sewer anywhere in town, so every drain ends at a tank

Guilford's plan of conservation and development states that the town has no sewers, and the health department says all sewage is disposed of on-site. In North Guilford that means every drain line ends at a septic tank and field, and the town's five-year pump-out rule applies to each one.

The same plan notes that septic effluent has contaminated nearby wells in some parts of town. It does not say which neighborhoods. When a North Guilford house has a bad taste, a smell or a drain problem, a plumber looks at the well and the septic system together, because they sit on the same lot. Our sewer line cleaning page explains the drain side.

Where North Guilford is, according to local sources

A local preservation group describes North Guilford as the village north of the Green, settled in the early 1700s and first called Cohabit. It grew around the North Guilford Congregational Church and St. John's Episcopal Church, and it includes the Guilford Lakes area, a chain of three lakes developed for vacation homes in 1929.

The Meeting House Hill historic district sits on Ledge Hill Road, about six miles from the town center by the nomination's account. Lake Quonnipaug is a 98.7-acre lake with a state boat launch on Durham Road, just east of Route 77. These are landmarks. They say nothing about the plumbing of nearby houses.

Cold weather and a house on a well

NOAA's 1991 to 2020 normals for a Mount Carmel station show a January average low of 20.2 degrees Fahrenheit. That is the closest inland record we used. The coastal New Haven airport record shows 23.0 degrees. North Guilford has no station in this data. Read the gap as a hint that inland winters run colder, not as a local measurement.

Connecticut's building code uses a 42-inch frost depth, which is a statewide figure. For a well house, what freezes first is usually a pressure tank in an unheated corner or a supply line near an outside wall. See burst pipe repair for what to do when one splits.

Before we arrive: if water is escaping

A burst pipe on a well-water house stops flowing when the pump is off, so the order of steps is different from a town-water house.

Switch off the well pump breaker

The pump keeps pushing water into a broken pipe as long as it has power and the tank has pressure.

Open a tap to relieve pressure

Once the pump is off, an open tap lets the tank empty so the leak slows.

Keep water away from the septic system

If the house drains are slow, limit water use until the cause is known.

What this page does not claim

We found no town or state source that says how many North Guilford houses use private wells, so we do not give a number. Statewide, the Department of Public Health says about 23 percent of Connecticut residents are served by private wells. That figure covers the whole state.

The state's public water list shows two small groundwater systems in Guilford named Quonnipaug Hills. The list does not say which streets they serve. Ask your neighbors or your landlord whether your house is connected.
