What Guilford Center means on this page

The National Park Service lists the Guilford Historic Town Center as a National Register district. Its nomination describes a district bounded by the West River, Interstate 95, East Creek and Long Island Sound, built around a 7.7-acre Green established in 1639.

The town center also has a local historic district, listed by the state's local historic district directory as established in 1987. For plumbing, the exact boundary matters less than the pattern it describes: older buildings, additions made over centuries and marsh edges on three sides. If you are unsure whether your address is inside, ask the town before assuming.

Septic in the middle of town

Guilford's health department states that sewage disposal is handled on-site by individual septic systems and community disposal systems. Every system is required to be pumped every five years, and based on lot size and soil, several areas of town must be inspected every year. The department's page also lists a study titled "Hydraulic Impact on Conventional Septic Systems in the Town Center." We have not read the study, and nothing on this page depends on it.

In practice, a backup near the Green can start in the house line, the tank or the field, and each has a different fix. Ask the health department whether your property is in a yearly-inspection area, then read our sewer line cleaning page for how a plumber separates the three.

Water service lines in older buildings

Connecticut Water's 2025 report for the Guilford System says some older homes and buildings may have service lines made of lead or of unknown material, and points customers to its service line lookup. The system-wide 2024 lead sampling it reports found 0 of 58 samples above the action level. That result is for the whole five-town system and says nothing about one address.

Start with the utility's lookup for your street. If the line to your building is old, damaged or of unknown material, our water service line repair page explains the work, and Connecticut's Call Before You Dig rule applies before any machine digs. Our leak detection service helps when a bill climbs and no wet spot shows.

Marsh edges, the West River and storm water

The nomination counts more than 250 acres of tidal wetlands along the district's edges. During Tropical Storm Irene in 2011, the National Weather Service record says heavy rain and coastal flooding put 2 to 3 feet of water over the roadway and properties around Water and River Streets along the West River. That is a record of one storm on one stretch of river, not a flood rating for any building.

FEMA maps Guilford with A, AE and VE flood zones, and the town requires a Flood Hazard Permit for many projects in those zones, including fuel tanks and generators. Check your address on FEMA's map, then ask the town's engineering department which permits a boiler, tank or generator needs. Where a basement takes on water, see sump pump service.

Signs that justify a call (general plumbing guidance)

These are common signs in any building. None of them is specific to Guilford Center.

  • ✓Several fixtures drain slowly at once. That points toward a shared line or tank. See drain cleaning.
  • ✓A sewage smell in the basement or near a floor drain. Stop using water and call.
  • ✓A water bill that rises with no visible leak. Shut off every fixture and watch the meter.
  • ✓Stains under a bathroom or on a kitchen ceiling. Water can travel along framing before it shows.
