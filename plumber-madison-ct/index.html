In Madison, the drain leads to a septic tank

Madison's 2024 plan of conservation and development says wastewater in town "consists almost exclusively of individual septic systems." The town's Water Pollution Control Authority has run a sewer avoidance program since the early 1980s, aimed at keeping sewers and a treatment plant from being needed.

For a homeowner, that means a slow drain can be a pipe problem inside the house or a full tank and a stressed leach field outside it. The two need different fixes, and snaking a line that leads to a failing system only delays the problem.

A plumber on a Madison call asks early whether the house is on septic, when the tank was last pumped and whether several fixtures are slow at once. See our sewer line cleaning page for how that triage works.

Emergency dispatch for Madison runs around the clock, so a backup at midnight does not wait for morning.

The five-year pump-out rule and what it means for emergency calls

The town's WPCA page states that all septic systems are required by ordinance to be pumped out every five years and that the pump-out is the homeowner's responsibility. Pumpers report each tank pumped, and the town notifies owners who are overdue.

An overdue tank is a common hidden cause of gurgling toilets, slow tubs and sewage smell in a basement. If you cannot remember the last pump-out, say so on the call. Pumping is a separate trade, but the plumber can tell whether the problem is inside the house, in the line to the tank or in the tank itself, and say when to call a pumper.

Madison's shoreline neighborhoods and flooding

The town's coastal resilience planning names neighborhoods such as Green Hill Place, Garnet Park, Circle Beach, Smith Bay, Surf Club Beach, West Wharf, Middle Beach and Webster Point as areas of concern for coastal flooding. The regional hazard mitigation plan records Madison's history with the National Flood Insurance Program, including 587 total flood losses and about $11.3 million in claims payments as of June 2022.

What that means for plumbing: low-lying houses near the water are the ones that see wet crawlspaces, buried drain lines under pressure from groundwater and sump pumps that run hard. If your home is in one of those areas, a backup sump pump and a check valve on the drain are worth discussing before the next big storm.

Winterizing a seasonal cottage in Madison

Madison has a Winterization Ordinance. Before a building permit is issued to convert a seasonal house to year-round use, the town requires an engineered, code-compliant septic system suited to the finished house, and the town says not every property can be winterized.

That makes the plumbing side of conversion a project, not a weekend job. Supply pipes that once sat in an unheated cottage need to be protected, a hot water system sized for year-round use is needed, and drains have to be checked against what the septic system can handle. Our plumbing installation service covers the planning, and the building department sets the permit steps.

Before we arrive: if you have a burst pipe or an active leak

Madison homes run from shoreline cottages to hillside houses on wells, and a plumbing emergency starts the same way in all of them.

Close the main, and the pump if you have a well

Turn the main valve clockwise. On a house with a private well, also switch off the pump breaker so it stops feeding the leak.

Stop the water going to the septic tank

If sewage is involved, stop using every fixture. A full tank and field cannot take extra water during a backup.

Keep power off wet floors

Only switch a breaker off from dry ground. In a flooded shoreline basement, leave the panel alone and tell the dispatcher.

Call and give the details

Your street, whether you are on public water or a well, and roughly when the tank was last pumped.
